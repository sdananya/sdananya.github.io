At midnight, someone outside her window was frying something in oil, and she had nothing to eat. It had been raining earlier when she finished moving in, or maybe it had just stopped. The floor smelled faintly of damp cardboard. She had three boxes, two plants, and no lighter for the gas stove. Not that it mattered. She couldn’t cook much anyway.

She turned on the exhaust fan instead of the kitchen light, then the balcony light instead of the fan. It felt like the house was playing a quiet game with her, and she lost every round.

The city outside her window never really slept. Even at midnight, someone somewhere was honking, laughing, letting a pressure cooker whistle. The noise felt like company at first. Then it just became noise.

A few weeks later, she noticed a cat near her gate. Orange, dusty, arrogant in a way only strays can be. It blinked at her like it was unimpressed by her entire existence. She liked it immediately. She started leaving out milk in a chipped bowl she found in the kitchen. Sometimes it drank, sometimes it didn’t. The bowl still waited every evening on the same step. She liked that too.

The days were long. Office, calls, the commute, and very little time for anything else. She told people she was “settling in,” though she wasn’t sure what that meant anymore. Some nights she played her ukulele softly until her fingers hurt. Other nights she drew the same plant over and over, never quite getting the shape of the leaves right.

There was a man she often passed on the staircase. He lived one floor up. Once he was carrying a broken chair, another time a stack of old newspapers. They didn’t speak initially. Then one evening she came home after dark and pressed a switch outside her door. Her own doorbell rang inside the empty flat. He was coming down the stairs and stopped a few steps above her.  
“Third one,” he said.  
The stairwell went yellow. She laughed, embarrassed, and he laughed too.

He was quiet, kind, a little unsure of himself. He never said clever things, but he showed up with an extra umbrella whenever it looked like rain. They started talking in the evenings, first in the corridor, later at the tea shop downstairs.  
The first time they went there, it rained without warning. He somehow had an extra umbrella. “I thought it might rain,” he said shyly. They stayed until the rain stopped anyway, two cups each, arguing about whether the vada pav stall near the park was better than the one by the bus stop. He said the bus stop one. He was wrong, and she told him so, and he just laughed without even challenging.

Some mornings she woke up and could breathe again. Other mornings the air itself felt too heavy, and she called in sick and lay on the floor under the fan, watching it turn. Her therapist said healing takes practice. She didn’t always believe her. On those days she didn’t go down for tea, and he never knocked to ask why. She was grateful for that, and a little disappointed.

The cat stopped showing up for a while. Then one day it came back thinner and louder, with a small kitten trailing behind. She left out two bowls of milk that evening.

In spring she saw cardboard boxes stacked at the bottom of the stairs, the broken chair balanced on top. She stood outside her door for a while. Then she went up and knocked. She suddenly realized she had never knocked on his door before, not once in all those months. He opened the door with tape in his hand and looked surprised, then embarrassed. She had meant to ask where he was going. Instead she asked if he needed help with the boxes. They packed his kitchen together without saying much. When she left, he pressed the spare umbrella into her hands.  
“It might rain,” he said. It didn’t, that night or for a week after.

By summer, the city had begun to feel softer around the edges. She still took wrong turns, still burned the toast, still forgot where she kept her socks. But she had favourite trees now, favourite corners in the park, a favourite smell in the air after rain, and a favourite song for long showers. She bought a lighter and learned to make one decent dal.

One evening, months after she first arrived, she came home late. She dropped her bag, reached for the wall without looking, and flicked the exact switch she needed. The fan started, slow and steady.

She didn’t even notice at first. Then she smiled.

Outside, traffic hummed and lights blinked in a rhythm that no longer felt alien. Inside, the air moved gently, circling the small room that had finally learned her name.
